Venue Capacity: What the Numbers Mean

Every venue profile on Codex Venue lists more than one capacity figure, because the number on the fire marshal's sign is rarely the number of tickets you should sell. This guide explains each figure and shows you how to work out a sellable count for any room.

Quick answer: A venue's licensed capacity is the most people the room may legally hold, including staff and performers. The number of tickets you put on sale is usually 10 to 20 percent lower once you account for crew, guest list, holds and how the room is set up that night.

The five capacity numbers you will see

Legal limit

Licensed occupancy

The maximum set by the local fire authority and posted in the room. It covers everyone inside, including bartenders, security, crew and the band.

Layout

Standing capacity

How many guests fit with an open floor and no chairs. This is the figure most club and general admission shows are booked against.

Layout

Seated capacity

How many guests fit with chairs or tables set. Expect it to be 30 to 50 percent lower than standing in the same room.

Sales

Sellable capacity

The number of tickets the venue allows on sale after removing staff, comps and holds. Talent buyers sometimes call this the ticket manifest.

Experience

Comfortable capacity

The crowd size where fans can still reach the bar and restrooms without fighting through the room. Our editors list this as the "recommended sell" on each profile.

What shrinks a room's real capacity

  • Production footprint. A larger stage, a drum riser, a front-of-house platform or a lighting tower can each remove dozens of standing spots. Our venue backline guide covers how touring gear affects stage space.
  • Barricades. A barricade and photo pit push the crowd back from the stage and reduce floor space.
  • All-ages shows. Roping off a bar area or adding a wristband zone usually cuts sellable capacity by around 10 percent.
  • Accessible viewing areas. Reserved platforms and companion space are required at many venues, so subtract them before you set your ticket count. See our accessible concert venues list for examples.
  • Merch and VIP areas. Merch tables, meet-and-greet lines and VIP sections all occupy floor space.
  • Weather. Outdoor stages may lower their limits when part of the grounds is closed for rain or heat.

How to calculate how many tickets to sell

Start with the standing or seated capacity for the layout you are using, then subtract each of the following. Here is a worked example for a 450-capacity club.

StepWhat to subtractExampleRunning total
1Start with standing capacity for tonight's layoutOpen floor450
2Venue staff, security and crew working inside18 people432
3Performers and touring party on every act14 people418
4Guest list and comps from artists and venue30 spots388
5Production holds (riser, front-of-house, barricade)20 spots368
6Sell to comfortable capacity, not to the limitAbout 5 percent buffer350 tickets

Agree on this number with the venue in writing before tickets go on sale. Some venues count guest list against licensed occupancy and others count it separately, and the difference can decide whether a show is a sellout or a capacity violation.

Rules vary by location: Occupancy limits, and what counts toward them, are set by local fire and building codes. Always confirm the current posted capacity and any show-specific limits with the venue's production manager.

Capacity questions to put in your advance

  • What is the posted licensed occupancy, and does it include staff and performers?
  • What is the sellable capacity for this show's layout and age restriction?
  • Does guest list count against sellable capacity?
  • How many spots does the production footprint remove?
  • How many accessible viewing spaces and companion spots are reserved?
  • Who at the venue has final say on capacity on show day?
